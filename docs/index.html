Economic Pulse

Does consumer sentiment lead the economy?

Consumer spending is roughly 70% of U.S. GDP, so if how people feel about the economy reliably runs ahead of unemployment and growth — rather than just moving alongside them — it is a useful early signal. This tests that directly, with Federal Reserve data, cross-correlation and Granger causality.

Relationship strength, by how far ahead sentiment is measured

Sentiment is shifted back by 1, 3, 6 and 12 months and compared against what unemployment and GDP did at that later date. The left panel is how strongly the two move together; the right is whether past sentiment actually improves a forecast.

Every value is negative: higher sentiment precedes a lower reading. Bars further from zero mean a stronger relationship.

Granger causality p-values on a log scale — lower is stronger evidence. Points below the 0.05 line mean past sentiment improves the forecast beyond the target's own history.

Seeing the lead directly

Two separate panels sharing one timeline — never one chart with two scales, which would invent an alignment that isn't in the data. Shift sentiment forward and watch whether its turning points start landing on unemployment's.

Shift sentiment forward by:

The pattern being tested: sentiment turning down before unemployment turns up. Shifting sentiment forward slides its peaks and troughs rightward — if the relationship is real, they should line up better with unemployment's at some shift than at none.
